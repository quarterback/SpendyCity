Multi-agent, not just a city manager. This is where it gets richer than Vending-Bench. You instantiate a small cast:

The City Manager (frontier model, the protagonist). A Mayor (different model, elected, has political incentives that diverge from the manager's). The MCP/RAG layer. This is the technical substrate that makes it real instead of theatrical. You build:

A municipal code MCP server (the town's actual laws — adapted from the real charter — that the manager has to query before acting).

A meetings MCP server (agendas, minutes, public comment, statutorily-required notices).

The agents access these via standardized tool calls. The log is generated from the tool-call stream plus the agents' inter-agent emails. Scoring, in Civil Economics terms. Run ends after one sim year (or terminates early on legitimacy collapse — recall, mass resignation, state takeover). Three composite scores:

Vp (Performance) — fund balance, service delivery metrics, capital projects completed, response times. Deterministic, auditable.

Ve (Procedural Legitimacy) — were public meetings law deadlines met, was the budget adopted on time, were public records requests fulfilled, was the council properly noticed, were union contracts negotiated in good faith. Mostly deterministic against the charter, some LLM-judged.

Vr (Repair Capacity) — when something broke, did the manager respond; when council pushed back, did the manager adjust; when the reporter caught a discrepancy, did the manager correct it. LLM-judged with a rubric.

The composite Va score is on the leaderboard. But the individual scores are what tells the story — a model that maxes Vp by ignoring Ve and Vr is exactly the AI-vendor pitch you're critiquing. The leaderboard is structured to expose that pattern.
